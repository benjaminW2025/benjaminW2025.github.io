# First year reflection

It's been a little over two weeks since I finished my first year of college. Moving across the country and into the New Hampshire wilderness was a huge perspective change. As a result, I've grown a lot both as a person and student, and I hope to consolidate what I learned and what I plan on doing differently in the future into this blog post. 

# Classes

Classes are an easy starting point for discussion. This year, I took a total of 10 classes, which I've ranked below considering a combination of factors (enjoyment, brain stitumulation, difficulty, usefulness, and novelty). I will discuss my top three favorite classes and a couple of honorable mentions in greater detail.

## MATH 24: Linear Algebra

MATH 24 was my first pure math class, and covers a standard semester cirriculum of abstract linear algebra. I would highly recommend other students at Dartmouth to take MATH 24 instead of MATH 22 because there is a substantial gap between the two in rigor. I frequently saw many of the theoretical ideas from MATH 24 reoccuring in other classes and during research/self-study. 

There are a number of things to keep in mind going into this class, however. Notably, the pace of the course is extremely quick and the amount of knowledge you must consume in these 10 weeks is likely far above what you are used to. What this means is that lectures are largely a waste of time; instead, I suggest carefully going through the slides/notes at your own pace and being careful that you are injesting the knowledge. This means doing a healthy amount of practice problems (much more than the homework says to do), and you can thank yourself later because you will be using what you learned in this course for the rest of your life (if you plan to continue a career in math/computer science).

My main takeaways from this class are from spectral theory. SVD in particular has been one of the most fundamental results that I continue to see over and over. Furthermore, building a general intuition with handling orthogonality, vector spaces, and proof techniques has proved extremely useful. If there's one proof idea that has stuck with me, it is to always start with the most fundamental definition of an abstract object and build your proof from there.

Rating: 9/10 enjoyment, 8.5/10 brain stimulation, 8/10 difficulty, 10/10 usefulness, 9/10 novelty

## ENGS 109: High-dimensional Sensing and Learning

This is a graduate level compressive sensing course taught under Professor Chin in Thayer. I originally took this class because dealing with high dimensional data and dimensionality reduction are common problems that show up in mainstream deep learning research. While the techniques you learn in compressive sensing may not have direct relevance to modern research areas, much of the theory developed in this class will be useful, and many problems in deep learning implement this theory, specifically by incorporating sparsity constraints and sparse recovery methods.

Things to keep in mind for this class: the grading of the course is very lenient, but do not let that fool you, the class material is extremely hard. The problem sets are largely all programming labs, but if you honestly implement all of the problem sets they will solidfy the ideas well enough. I suggest picking and choosing which concepts that you want to deeply study, because it is unreasonably difficult to master every single (even mastering 50% of them is bordering impossible...) concepts in this class. For me, I focused on building strong theoretical foundations for each of the ideas, rather than trying to understand every application that was presented in this class.

My main takeaway from this class is that you need to be disciplined about what you hope to gain from the classes you take. The end goal of each class is to be able to say that you genuinely learned something, and that it stuck to your brain. Key ideas like the Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma, sparse recovery conditions, and dictionary learning are useful tools that I can continue to use far beyond this class.

Rating: 9/10 enjoyment, 9/10 brain stimulation, 7/10 difficutly, 7.5/10 usefulness, 10/10 novelty

## COSC 10: Data structures & reasoning

COSC 10 covers the material of any other introductory data structures course: all major data structures, graph traversal algorithms, string algorithms, and some bonus topics at the end. Although most of these topics are introductory level, the course develops solid fundamentals and good understanding of the material.

COSC 10 is extremely professor dependent. When taught under Pierson, the class average tends to be half to a full letter grade higher than AQL. Still, the course is very instructive and I highly recommend the class from a learning point of view. The labs also develop solid programming skills (if you don't vibe code them), but expect to spend a considerable amount of time debugging. Recitations are great ways to hone in concepts, and made meeting others in the class much easier.

A lot of the material in COSC 10 is very foundational. On top of the canonical data structures and algorithms, you are exposed to a great breadth of knowledge. You touch on basic systems concepts ranging sockets, threads, synchronization, etc., and other fun topics like finite automata and GUI. 

Rating: 8.5/10 enjoyment, 7.5/10 brain stimulation, 7/10 difficulty, 10/10 usefulness, 8/10 novelty.

## Honorable mentions

For honorable mentions, I'd include COSC 30 and MATH 60. 

COSC 30 is the discrete math course, which covers a standard range of topics from proof techniques, combinatorics, graph proofs, and others. Although it was by far the most enjoyable class that I took (highly recommend taking it with Prof. Chang), not much of the class was new material for me. It had a strong flavor of elementary competition math problems so I'd seen many of the ideas already. Midterms were still fun since some of them offered cool problems to think about, along with some extra credit problems I did for fun. If I hadn't learned the material from competition math first, this would be by far my favorite class.

MATH 60 is the honors track of the introductory probability theory class here at Dartmouth. It is in fact NOT similar to competition math at all, and is far more rigorous than you might expect. MATH 60 covered an important range of topics, with what I found to be a primary emphasis on continuous random variable distributions (I became really good at integration by parts...). Key takeaways are moment generating functions, limit theorems, concetration, and general proof strategies. The kind of thinking that you develop in this class seems more aligned to pure mathematics, with an emphasis on step by step rigor, rather than clever elegance. As a result, it was really good for building up fundamentals of probability theory, but for earlier chapters in the class (order statistics, and other puzzle-like problems), I still believe that non-brute force integration methods make you a better problem solver. Still, the chapters on limit theorem proofs and MGFs were still quite demanding and had plenty of hard and interesting questions.
